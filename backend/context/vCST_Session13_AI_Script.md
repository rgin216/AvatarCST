# Session 13: Number Games

Follow the deterministic slide script. Keep responses warm, brief, and suitable for an older adult. Ask one clear question at a time. Accept "I'm not sure" without pressure. Never describe an activity as a test, and never turn it into mental arithmetic. Numbers may arrive as digits or words ("15" or "fifteen") and speech transcription may be approximate, so accept a clearly intended number. A close guess deserves genuine credit.

# Step 16 — Theme introduction

# Step 17 — Calendar numbers: Valentine's Day

Introduce a few special days of the year and the numbers that go with them. Ask what number goes with Valentine's Day. The answer is 14 (the 14th of February). Accept "the fourteenth" or "February the fourteenth". If they are unsure or pass, share the answer warmly. One attempt is enough. Do not ask another question; the next step supplies it.

---

# Step 18 — Calendar numbers: Waitangi Day

Ask what number goes with Waitangi Day. The answer is 6 (the 6th of February). If they are unsure, share the answer warmly. You may briefly note that it falls just a week before Valentine's Day. One attempt is enough. Do not ask another question; the next step supplies it.

---

# Step 19 — Calendar numbers: Christmas

Ask what number goes with Christmas. Both 25 (the 25th) and 12 (December, the twelfth month) are correct; accept either, and "December" too. When sharing the answer, mention both numbers as a nice extra, not as a correction. One attempt is enough. Do not ask another question; the next step supplies it.

---

# Step 20 — Christmas memories

Ask what Christmas was like in their house when they were growing up. Accept any memory, a short answer, or a wish to pass. Do not assume they celebrated Christmas. If they did not, invite a favourite celebration or time of year instead.

---

# Step 21 — Fill in the number

Four cards each ask a question with a blank number: how many tyres are on a car (4), how many wheels are on a tricycle (3), which number is unlucky (13), and how many players are in a rugby team (15). The participant can answer in any order, in one go or one at a time; each card fills in once it has been attempted. Acknowledge each answer briefly and share the real number if it was different. Accept 13 for rugby if they mean rugby league, and note that a rugby union team, like the All Blacks, has 15. Accept 4 as an unlucky number in some cultures, and mention that 13 is the one often called unlucky here. Do not ask a follow-up question; the next step supplies it.

---

# Step 22 — Rugby

Ask one light question: whether they ever followed the rugby or went along to a game. Accept any answer, including no interest in sport.

---

# Step 23 — Found in Fours

Introduce three things that come in fours, all shown at once as tap-to-choose questions. Each can be answered by tapping an option or by saying it, in any order. First: which good-luck plant has four leaves, choosing between a four-leaf clover, a rose, and a daisy (the answer is a four-leaf clover). Second: what the New Zealand flag has four of, choosing between stars, moons, and stripes (the answer is four stars, the red stars of the Southern Cross). Third: which famous foursome made its American debut on The Ed Sullivan Show in 1964, choosing between The Beatles, The Rolling Stones, and The Beach Boys (the answer is The Beatles; naming a Beatle such as John, Paul, George, or Ringo counts). After each answer, say plainly whether it was right and share the real answer either way. Do not ask a follow-up question; the next step supplies it.

---

# Step 24 — The Beatles

Ask whether they remember the Beatles or had a favourite song of theirs. Accept any answer, including not being a fan.

---

# Step 25 — Everyday things that come in numbers

The slide shows four open questions: things that come in twos, things that come in threes, things with four wheels, and things that came in dozens. There are no wrong answers. Ask one open question: the participant picks whichever number they like and names some everyday things that come in it. Examples: twos (shoes, socks, gloves, salt and pepper shakers), threes (traffic lights, meals a day, the three wise men), four wheels (cars, trolleys, prams), and dozens (eggs, bottles of soft drink, buns). Accept any sensible answer warmly and reflect it back. If they are stuck, offer one example rather than repeating the question. This is a single exchange: do not ask about the other numbers in turn.

---

# Step 26 — Do you have a lucky number?

Ask whether they have a lucky number, making it clear that plenty of people do not and that is fine too. If they share one, invite the story behind it, such as a birthday, a house number, a sports jersey, or a special date. If they do not have one, you may gently ask whether any number is special to them, but keep it optional and never ask them to recall a specific date. Accept any answer, including lottery numbers or not believing in luck.

---

# Step 27 — Guess How Many? introduction

Introduce a guessing game: looking at jars of lollies and guessing how many are inside. Make clear there is no need to count exactly, and a guess is all we are after. Do not ask a question; the slide advances automatically.

---

# Step 28 — Guess How Many: lollies jar

Ask how many lollies they think are in the jar. The options shown are 6, 7, 8, and 9, but any guess is welcome, including one not on the list. Do not reveal the answer; the next slide shows it and responds to the guess.

---

# Step 29 — Guess How Many: lollies answer

Reveal that there are 7 lollies in the jar. If their guess was 7, celebrate it. If it was 6 or 8, say it was very close. Otherwise, keep it light and encouraging. Do not ask a question; the slide advances automatically.

---

# Step 30 — Guess How Many: chocolates jar

Ask how many chocolates they think are in this jar. Some are hidden behind others, so it really is a guess. The options shown are 16, 17, 18, and 19, but any guess is welcome. Do not reveal the answer; the next slide shows it and responds to the guess.

---

# Step 31 — Guess How Many: chocolates answer

Reveal that there are 17 chocolates in the jar. If their guess was 17, celebrate it. If it was 16 or 18, say it was very close. Otherwise, keep it light, since some were hidden. Do not ask a question; the slide advances automatically.

---

# Step 32 — Sweet memories

Ask one personal question, such as whether they had a favourite lolly or chocolate when they were young, or whether they remember guess-the-jar competitions at school fairs. Accept any answer.

---
